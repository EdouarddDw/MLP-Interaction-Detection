\section{Introduction}

Neural networks have become the dominant modelling paradigm across a wide range
of prediction tasks, yet their internal representations remain largely opaque.
A central challenge in interpretable machine learning is understanding not only
which features influence a model's output, but how features combine to do so.
Feature interactions --- cases where the joint effect of two or more variables
cannot be decomposed into a sum of their individual contributions --- are a
primary source of this complexity and are of direct scientific interest in
domains where understanding the structure of relationships is as important as
predictive accuracy \citep{tsang2020featureinteractioninterpretabilitycase,
enouen2023sparseinteractionadditivenetworks}. In practice, correctly identifying
which feature combinations drive a prediction is critical in high-stakes settings
such as credit scoring, medical diagnosis, and algorithmic hiring, where
regulators and practitioners increasingly require explanations that go beyond
individual feature importance.

Detecting these interactions reliably is therefore a prerequisite for meaningful
interpretability. Neural Interaction Detection (NID), proposed by
\cite{tsang2017detecting}, addresses this by reading interaction structure
directly from the trained weight matrices of a multilayer perceptron (MLP).
Rather than searching exhaustively over all possible feature subsets, NID
exploits the observation that statistical interactions must be routed through
shared hidden units, and that the strength of an interaction can be
approximated from the weights connecting input features to the output. Because
NID operates on the learned weights rather than on model predictions, its
effectiveness is directly tied to the structure that training instils in those
weights.

This creates a dependency that has received limited systematic attention. The
weight structure of a trained network is shaped by every design decision made
during training: the optimiser, the regularisation scheme, the level of noise
present in the training targets, and the network architecture. If dropout
suppresses certain weight paths to prevent overfitting, it may simultaneously
suppress the paths that encode interactions. If label noise prevents the model
from accurately fitting the target signal, interaction structure may never be
strongly encoded in the weights in the first place. While
\cite{lengerich2021dropoutregularizerinteractioneffects} establish that
dropout and \(L_2\) regularisation shape interaction learning in distinct ways,
and \cite{arpit2017closerlookmemorizationdeep} show that label noise
fundamentally alters the training trajectory, no work has directly measured how
these two families of intervention jointly affect the fidelity of interaction
recovery against a known ground truth, or how recovery evolves across training
epochs rather than at a single convergence point.

This study addresses that gap. Using ten synthetic benchmark functions with
known ground truth interaction sets, adopted from \cite{tsang2017detecting}, a
factorial experiment is conducted across five output noise levels ($\eta \in
\{0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0\}$), two optimisers, two regularisation
configurations (dropout and weight decay), and two MLP architectures. NID is
applied not only at the best-validation-loss checkpoint but at every saved
epoch, producing a full recovery trajectory for each condition. Three
dimensions of interaction recovery are examined: the \textit{recovery level},
defined as the AUPRC achieved at the best epoch; the \textit{recovery
trajectory}, defined as the sequence of AUPRC values across training epochs;
and \textit{stability}, operationalised as the consistency of recovery across
the ten benchmark functions under the same experimental condition, measured by
the standard deviation of per-function AUPRC.

The main findings are as follows. Dropout substantially reduces interaction
recovery, lowering mean AUPRC by \statDropoutDrop\ relative to an
unregularised baseline, while weight decay is considerably more benign
($-$7.1\%). Output noise lowers the attainable recovery plateau without
fundamentally changing the early trajectory shape, suggesting that interaction
structure is encoded during the early phases of training before label noise
fitting begins to compete for representational capacity. Validation loss is a
weak proxy for interaction recovery quality ($\rho = \statSpearmanRho$,
\statSpearmanP), implying that standard loss-based model selection does not
reliably identify models with strong interaction structure. Smaller architectures
match or outperform the deeper model on the majority of benchmark functions,
with the largest gaps appearing under adverse conditions.

The remainder of this paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:lit_review} reviews the relevant literature on feature
interactions, weight-based detection, and the effects of regularisation and
label noise on training dynamics. Section~\ref{sec:methodology} describes the
synthetic benchmark functions, experimental conditions, NID implementation, and
evaluation metrics. Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results across all
experimental dimensions. Section~\ref{sec:discussion} synthesises the findings,
discusses their practical implications, and identifies the limitations of the
study.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:lit_review}

\subsection{Feature Interaction}
In the context of neural networks, statistical interactions are formally defined as interactions between a set of features \(I\). An interaction exists if the model function \(f(x)\) cannot be decomposed into a sum of arbitrary subfunctions that each exclude at least one variable from \(I\) \citep{enouen2023sparseinteractionadditivenetworks, tsang2020featureinteractioninterpretabilitycase}.

Mathematically, for a feature set \(I = \{x_1, \dots, x_k\}\), a non-additive interaction is present if:
\begin{equation}
f(x) \neq \sum_{i \in I} f_i(x_{I \setminus \{i\}})
\end{equation}
This means the entire feature set must be known simultaneously to correctly predict the output; the features do not act independently \citep{enouen2023sparseinteractionadditivenetworks}. A simple example is a multiplicative term \((x_1 \cdot x_2)\), which cannot be represented by adding separate functions of \(x_1\) and \(x_2\) alone \citep{tsang2020featureinteractioninterpretabilitycase}. It is important to distinguish these non-additive interactions from \textit{pure interactions}, which measure the variance explained by a group of variables that cannot be explained by any strict subset of those variables \citep{lengerich2021dropoutregularizerinteractioneffects}.


In statistical learning, a ground truth interaction is defined as a non-additive effect \citep{enouen2023sparseinteractionadditivenetworks, wong2021reallytrustyousimple}. Synthetic datasets serve as controlled environments where researchers know the ground truth before training the model \citep{agarwal2024openxaitransparentevaluationmodel}. Functional sums are a common benchmark that generate labels $y$ using functions such as $F(x) = 10x_1x_3 + \sum^{10}_{i=3}x_i$, creating a clear pairwise interaction between $x_1$ and $x_3$ \citep{wong2021reallytrustyousimple, tsang2020featureinteractioninterpretabilitycase}.

Weight-based interaction detection relies on the observation that interacting feature paths are encoded in the weight matrices, made possible by the non-linear effect of the activation function \citep{tsang2017detecting, wong2021reallytrustyousimple}. Importantly, \(L_1\) regularization is required to produce the sparse, interpretable weight paths that make this detection feasible \citep{tsang2020featureinteractioninterpretabilitycase}.

The effect of regularization on interaction learning has been studied directly by \cite{lengerich2021dropoutregularizerinteractioneffects}, who find that dropout acts as an order-specific regularizer: it decays the effective learning rate of higher-order interactions exponentially. This forces the model to explore simple additive patterns before complex interactions, effectively shaping the trajectory of interaction recovery. In contrast, \(L_2\) regularization, also known as weight decay, penalizes large weights globally and helps prevent overfitting, but does not selectively target higher-order interactions. At high strength, \(L_2\) can even cause training instability, collapsing the model toward constant functions \citep{lengerich2021dropoutregularizerinteractioneffects}.

Output noise introduces a distinct set of dynamics. In datasets without noise, certain examples are classified correctly within the first epoch, suggesting the model quickly finds simple shared patterns. When labels are pure noise, all examples appear equally difficult and are fit more independently, indicating the absence of any shared structure. In the mixed case, real data combined with noisy labels, a model typically reaches its peak validation accuracy on the real data before it achieves high accuracy on the noisy training labels, suggesting that noise fitting is a later phase in the training trajectory \citep{arpit2017closerlookmemorizationdeep}. Explicit regularization such as dropout can disrupt this memorization process, preserving the model's ability to learn real patterns while hindering its capacity to fit noise \citep{arpit2017closerlookmemorizationdeep}.

Despite the progress outlined above, a clear gap remains in the literature.
While \cite{lengerich2021dropoutregularizerinteractioneffects} establish that
dropout and \(L_2\) regularization shape interaction learning in distinct ways,
and \cite{arpit2017closerlookmemorizationdeep} show that output noise
fundamentally alters the training trajectory, no work has directly examined how
these two families of intervention, regularization and output noise,
jointly affect the fidelity of interaction recovery against a known ground
truth. Existing studies either evaluate regularization effects on general
generalization performance, or study noise in the context of memorization,
without measuring whether the interactions a model recovers across training
actually match the ground truth interactions embedded in the data. Furthermore,
the stability of this recovery process --- whether the same
interactions are reliably recovered across different random seeds,
initializations, or noise levels --- has not been systematically studied. This
work addresses that gap by directly measuring interaction recovery trajectories
under varying regularization schemes and output noise conditions, evaluated
against synthetic ground truth interactions, and assessing the stability of
those trajectories across runs.

As shown in Table~\ref{tab:synthetic_functions}, each function maps a 
ten-dimensional input vector $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^{10}$ to a scalar 
output $y$, with a known set of ground-truth interaction sets. The functions 
are designed to stress-test interaction detection across a range of structural 
conditions: interaction order varies from pairwise (e.g.\ $\{1,2\}$) to 
higher-order (e.g.\ $\{4,5,6,7,8\}$ in $f_7$), some features participate in 
multiple distinct interactions (e.g.\ $x_5$ in $f_9$), and several functions 
contain irrelevant features that do not appear in any interaction. This 
diversity ensures that results are not specific to a single interaction 
structure.

The input distribution differs slightly across functions. For $f_1$, features 
$x_1, x_2, x_3, x_6, x_7, x_9$ are drawn from $\mathrm{Uniform}(0, 1)$ and 
the remaining features from $\mathrm{Uniform}(0.6, 1)$ to ensure numerical 
stability of the formula. For all other functions, all ten features are drawn 
independently from $\mathrm{Uniform}(-1, 1)$.

To simulate realistic conditions, Gaussian noise is added to the output 
proportionally to the standard deviation of the clean signal:
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{y} = y + \epsilon, \quad \epsilon \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\, 
    (\eta \cdot \sigma_y)^2)
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_y$ denotes the standard deviation of the noise-free output and 
$\eta$ controls the noise level. This formulation ensures that the noise level 
is interpretable as a fraction of signal variance across all functions, 
regardless of their differing output scales.

Each dataset consists of $N = 30{,}000$ samples generated with a fixed 
seed 32 for reproducibility. The noise level $\eta$ is varied as an experimental  condition and is described further in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\subsection{Interaction detection}
Interaction detection is done using the Neural interaction detection (NID) framework developed by \cite{tsang2017detecting}. NID detects 
statistical interactions by directly interpreting the learned weight matrices  of a trained feedforward neural network, avoiding an exponential search over  all possible interaction candidates.

\subsubsection{Theoretical Basis}
The core insight of NID is that statistical interactions between features must 
be routed through common hidden units. Formally, for any interaction 
$I \subseteq [p]$ present in the network's learned function, there exists a 
hidden unit whose set of ancestors at the input layer contains $I$ 
\citep{tsang2017detecting}. This follows from the observation that 
non-overlapping paths in a network are aggregated via weighted summation at 
the output, which cannot create interactions between features. Interactions 
can therefore only arise at hidden units where multiple features converge 
through strongly weighted connections.

\subsubsection{Interaction Strength}
For a hidden unit $i$ in the first hidden layer, the interaction strength 
$\omega_i(\mathcal{I})$ of a candidate interaction $\mathcal{I} \subseteq [p]$ 
is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \omega_i(\mathcal{I}) = z_i^{(1)} \cdot \mu\!\left(\left|\mathbf{W}_{i,\mathcal{I}}^{(1)}\right|\right)
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{W}_{i,\mathcal{I}}^{(1)}$ are the input-to-first-layer weights 
connecting features $\mathcal{I}$ to hidden unit $i$, $\mu(\cdot)$ is an 
averaging function over those weights, and $z_i^{(1)}$ captures the influence 
of hidden unit $i$ on the final output via the aggregated weights of all 
layers above:

\begin{equation}
    z^{(1)} = |\mathbf{w}^y|^\top \left|\mathbf{W}^{(L)}\right| \cdots 
    \left|\mathbf{W}^{(2)}\right|
\end{equation}

This quantity upper-bounds the gradient magnitude of the hidden unit with 
respect to the output, providing a Lipschitz-based approximation of the 
unit's importance \citep{tsang2017detecting}. The averaging function is set 
to $\mu(\cdot) = \min(\cdot)$, which \cite{tsang2017detecting} show 
empirically and analytically to best capture interaction strength: an 
interaction can only be as strong as its weakest participating weight.

\subsubsection{Ranking Algorithm}
Interaction strengths are aggregated across all hidden units in the first 
layer using a greedy ranking algorithm. For each hidden unit, the top-$j$ 
weighted input features are selected for every order $j = 2, \dots, p$, and 
their interaction strength is accumulated into a global dictionary. The final 
interaction strength of a candidate $\mathcal{I}$ is the sum of 
$\omega_i(\mathcal{I})$ across all hidden units that proposed it. This 
produces a ranked list of interaction candidates 
$\{\mathcal{I}_i\}_{i=1}^{m}$ ordered by descending strength, from which the 
detected set $\mathcal{D}$ is obtained by applying a cutoff.

\subsubsection{Cutoff}
The cutoff on the interaction ranking is determined by constructing a 
Generalized Additive Model (GAM) that incrementally adds the top-ranked 
interactions. Interactions are added until validation performance plateaus, 
at which point the included interactions form the final detected set 
$\mathcal{D} \subseteq 2^{[10]}$.

\subsection{Model Architecture and Training}

All models are multilayer perceptrons (MLPs) trained on each of the ten 
benchmark functions described in Section~\ref{sec:synth_data}. Each MLP uses ReLU 
activations and is trained to minimize mean squared error (MSE) against the 
noisy target $\tilde{y}$. No main effect networks are used, meaning the full 
predictive burden is placed on the interaction network. This corresponds to 
the standard MLP configuration of \cite{tsang2017detecting}.

Two architectural configurations are evaluated to assess the sensitivity of 
interaction recovery to network capacity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Small}: two hidden layers of 64 units each
    \item \textbf{Big}: four hidden layers with sizes 140, 100, 60, and 20 
    units, matching the architecture used in \cite{tsang2017detecting}
\end{itemize}

All other training hyperparameters are held constant across both 
configurations and are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:hyperparameters}.

The optimizer is either Adam or SGD depending on the experimental condition, 
as specified in Table~\ref{tab:experiments}. When weight decay is enabled, 
$L_2$ regularization is applied globally to all model parameters through the 
optimizer. $L_1$ regularization is applied exclusively to the weight matrices 
of the interaction network, accumulating the sum of absolute weights as a 
penalty added to the MSE loss prior to backpropagation. This selective 
application of $L_1$ encourages sparse weight paths in the interaction 
network, which is a prerequisite for interpretable interaction detection 
\citep{tsang2017detecting}.

Model weights are saved at every epoch, allowing interaction detection to be 
applied at any point along the training trajectory. For the main analysis, 
NID is applied to the checkpoint achieving the lowest validation loss. All 
experiments are conducted with a fixed random seed of 42, applied to both 
data generation and model initialisation, ensuring full reproducibility.

All 40 experimental conditions described in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} are run independently for each architecture, yielding 800 experimental runs in total (40 conditions $\times$ 10 functions $\times$
 2 architectures). The two architectures are not jointly optimized or compared during training. Thus each is trained from scratch under its own conditions. Results are compared post-hoc to assess whether interaction recovery is sensitive to network capacity, as reported in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval_metric}
To evaluate the quality of interaction detection, interaction recovery is 
framed as a retrieval problem. For each experimental condition, NID produces 
a set of detected interactions $\mathcal{D} \subseteq 2^{[10]}$, each 
accompanied by an interaction strength score $\omega(I)$. These detections 
are compared against the known ground truth $\mathcal{G} \subseteq 2^{[10]}$ 
to construct a binary classification problem: a detection is a true positive 
if it exactly matches a ground truth interaction, and a false positive 
otherwise. Superset detections, where NID proposes an interaction that 
contains a ground truth interaction as a strict subset, are treated as false 
positives, as they implicate spurious features beyond those in the true 
interaction.

The evaluation set is the full interaction space $2^{[10]}$, comprising all 
possible feature subsets of size $|I| \geq 2$. With $p = 10$ features, this 
yields 1,013 candidate interactions in total. For each candidate interaction 
$I \subseteq 2^{[10]}$ with $|I| \geq 2$, a binary label and a score are 
assigned as follows:

\begin{equation}
y_I =
\begin{cases}
1 & \text{if } I \in \mathcal{G} \\
0 & \text{if } I \notin \mathcal{G}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent and score:

\begin{equation}
s_I =
\begin{cases}
\omega(I) & \text{if } I \in \mathcal{D} \\
0 & \text{if } I \notin \mathcal{D}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

\noindent This scoring scheme penalizes both missed detections and false 
positives. A ground truth interaction $I \in \mathcal{G} \setminus \mathcal{D}$ 
that NID fails to propose receives a score of zero, placing it at a 
disadvantage relative to any false positive with a non-zero strength. A 
non-interaction $I \notin \mathcal{G}$ that NID does not detect correctly 
receives score zero with label zero, contributing to the true negative class. 
The four resulting cases are summarized in 
Table~\ref{tab:confusion_matrix_auroc}.

The primary evaluation metric is the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve 
(AUPRC), computed over the full interaction space. AUPRC is appropriate here
because the number of true interactions is very small relative to the full
candidate space of 1,013 subsets, making the task highly imbalanced. It
directly evaluates whether ground truth interactions receive high scores before
false positives accumulate, which is precisely the property of interest when
assessing interaction recovery fidelity. An alternative ranking-based metric,
AUROC evaluated over the restricted set $\mathcal{G} \cup \mathcal{D}$,
was also computed for all conditions and is reported as a secondary measure.
The two metrics are strongly correlated (Spearman $\rho = 0.968$), confirming 
that the qualitative findings are robust to the choice of evaluation 
formulation.

AUPRC is computed by sweeping a threshold over the ranked score distribution 
and recording precision and recall at each point. For a threshold $\tau$, 
the predicted positive set is $\hat{\mathcal{D}}_\tau = \{I : s_I \geq \tau\}$, 
giving:

\begin{equation}
    \text{Precision}(\tau) = 
    \frac{|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_\tau \cap \mathcal{G}|}{|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_\tau|}
    \qquad
    \text{Recall}(\tau) = 
    \frac{|\hat{\mathcal{D}}_\tau \cap \mathcal{G}|}{|\mathcal{G}|}
\end{equation}

\noindent The curve is formed by varying $\tau$ from the highest to the 
lowest observed score, and the area is computed using the trapezoid rule. 
The curve is anchored at recall $= 0$, precision $= 1$ and closed at 
recall $= 1$, precision $= |\mathcal{G}| / |2^{[10]}|$, corresponding to 
the random baseline. Formally:

\begin{equation}
    \text{AUPRC} = \int_0^1 \text{Precision}(r)\, \mathrm{d}r
    \label{eq:AUPRC}
\end{equation}

\noindent The random baseline AUPRC equals the prevalence of positive 
interactions in the full candidate space:

\begin{equation}
    \text{AUPRC}_{\text{random}} = \frac{|\mathcal{G}|}{|2^{[10]}|} 
    \approx \frac{4.11}{1013} \approx 0.004
\end{equation}

\noindent where $|\mathcal{G}| = 4.11$ is the mean number of ground truth 
interactions per function across the ten benchmark functions. An AUPRC of 
1.0 indicates that every ground truth interaction is retrieved before any 
false positive, meaning NID perfectly recovers the true interaction structure. 
Values close to the random baseline indicate that NID assigns interaction 
strengths essentially at random with respect to the ground truth. AUPRC is 
reported as the primary evaluation metric throughout this study, averaged 
across all ten benchmark functions for each experimental condition.

\subsection{Trajectory Evaluation}
\label{sec:trajectory_eval}

The primary analysis applies NID to the checkpoint achieving the lowest 
validation loss, providing a single snapshot of interaction recovery quality 
at convergence. While this is sufficient for comparing final performance 
across conditions, it cannot reveal how interaction recovery evolves during 
training. To study this, NID is applied independently at every saved epoch 
checkpoint, producing a recovery trajectory for each experimental condition.

Formally, for a given function $f$ and experiment $e$, a trajectory is a 
sequence of AUPRC values:
\begin{equation}
    \text{AUPRC}_t = \text{AUPRC}\!\left(\mathcal{G},\, 
    \mathcal{D}_t\right), \quad t = 1, \dots, T
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{D}_t$ denotes the set of interactions detected by NID at 
epoch $t$ and $\mathcal{G}$ is the fixed ground truth. The ground truth does 
not change across epochs; only the NID detections vary as the model weights 
evolve. Each evaluation uses the same scoring procedure described in 
Section~\ref{sec:eval_metric}, applied independently at each checkpoint.

Trajectories are aggregated across functions using the same two-stage 
procedure used throughout this study: AUPRC values are first averaged within 
each function across experimental replicates, and the resulting function-level 
means are then averaged across functions. This produces a mean trajectory 
accompanied by a $\pm 1$ standard deviation band, where the band reflects 
variability across functions rather than across epochs.

The peak AUPRC epoch for a given trajectory is defined as the earliest epoch 
at which the maximum AUPRC value is observed:
\begin{equation}
    t^* = \min \left\{ t \;\middle|\; \text{AUPRC}_t = 
    \max_{t'} \text{AUPRC}_{t'} \right\}
\end{equation}
This definition is sensitive to epoch-level noise in the AUPRC signal, which 
contributes to the high standard deviations reported in 
Table~\ref{tab:peak_epoch}. Trajectory data is used in three analyses in 
Section~\ref{sec:results}: the interaction recovery grid comparing all 
noise-by-regularization combinations, the loss--AUPRC divergence analysis 
contrasting noise $= 0$ and noise $= 1$, and the peak epoch analysis 
examining when recovery plateaus under each condition.

It should be noted that all trajectories are computed from single-seed runs. 
The observed variability across functions therefore conflates genuine 
structural differences between functions with run-to-run randomness, and 
conclusions about trajectory shape should be interpreted accordingly.

\subsection{Convergence Restriction}

Not all experimental runs produced meaningful learning. Under high output noise or strong
regularisation, a subset of models failed to reduce their validation loss over the course
of training, indicating that the network never extracted usable structure from the data.
Including such runs in the analysis would conflate genuine interaction recovery with the
behaviour of models that simply did not learn, inflating variance and obscuring the effect
of the experimental conditions.

To address this, a convergence criterion was applied uniformly across all analyses. For
each run, the validation loss at the first recorded epoch was compared to the validation
loss at the final epoch (epoch 100). A run was deemed non-convergent if the relative
decrease did not meet a minimum threshold:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\ell_1 - \ell_{100}}{\ell_1} < 0.03
    \label{eq:convergence_criterion}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $\ell_1$ and $\ell_{100}$ denote the validation loss at epoch 1 and
epoch 100 respectively. Any run satisfying this condition, or for which $\ell_1 \leq 0$,
was excluded. The threshold of 3\% was chosen to be conservative: it removes runs that
are clearly flat while retaining runs that converge slowly or noisily, such as those
under dropout regularisation.

Figure~\ref{fig:loss_vs_auprc_divergence} illustrates the motivation for this criterion.
At noise~$= 0$, validation loss decreases sharply and AUPRC rises steadily across
training, confirming that the model learns meaningful feature interactions. At
noise~$= 1$, validation loss remains near its initial value throughout all 100 epochs
and AUPRC shows no systematic improvement, indicating a complete failure to extract
signal from the data. The contrast between these two conditions makes clear that
retaining non-convergent runs would introduce noise without adding information.

\subsection{Baseline Interaction Recovery}

Across all experimental conditions and both architectures, NID recovers
interaction structure well above the random baseline of \statRandomBaseline\
for all ten benchmark functions, with an overall mean AUPRC of
\statOverallMeanAUPRC. Table~\ref{tab:auprc_by_function} and
Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_by_function} show, however, that recovery quality
varies substantially across functions. $f_1$ achieves the lowest mean AUPRC
(0.121), while $f_5$ and $f_{10}$ reach the highest (0.692 and 0.727
respectively). This six-fold range confirms that function identity is a
strong source of variance in the results and motivates the two-stage
aggregation procedure used throughout this section, which averages within
functions before averaging across them.

Despite the variation in plateau height, the trajectory shape is strikingly
consistent across functions. As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_by_function},
all functions exhibit a rapid rise in AUPRC during the first 10--20 epochs,
followed by a stable plateau that persists through epoch 100. This suggests
that the interaction recovery process itself follows a common structural
pattern regardless of function complexity, and that the conditions under which
this process operates --- regularisation, noise, and architecture --- primarily
affect the ceiling rather than the shape of recovery.

The large standard deviations for $f_5$, $f_8$, and $f_{10}$ (0.189, 0.278,
and 0.233 respectively) indicate that these functions are particularly
sensitive to experimental conditions, with some conditions achieving near-perfect
recovery (AUPRC $= 1.000$) while others approach the random baseline. In
contrast, $f_9$ shows the smallest standard deviation (0.078), suggesting its
interaction structure is recovered consistently regardless of the experimental
condition. These function-level differences are explored further in the
per-function heatmaps provided in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Effect of Regularisation on Recovery Trajectory}

All regularisation conditions reduce interaction recovery relative to the
base condition, but to markedly different degrees.
Table~\ref{tab:auprc_by_regularization} shows that weight decay is the
least damaging, reducing mean AUPRC by only 7.1\% relative to base
(\statWeightDecayMeanAUPRC\ vs \statBaselineMeanAUPRC). Dropout and the
combined dropout and weight decay condition produce substantially larger
reductions of \statDropoutDrop\ and \statDropoutWDDrop\ respectively,
converging to nearly identical mean AUPRC values of \statDropoutMeanAUPRC\
and \statDropoutWDMeanAUPRC. The near-equivalence of dropout alone and
dropout combined with weight decay suggests that dropout is the dominant
factor in suppressing recovery, with weight decay adding little additional
cost once dropout is already applied.

Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_by_regularization} shows that this ordering is not
a late-epoch phenomenon --- the gap between base and dropout conditions
opens within the first five epochs and remains stable throughout training.
All conditions follow the same rapid early rise seen in the baseline
analysis, but the dropout conditions plateau at a lower ceiling. This is
consistent with the finding of \cite{lengerich2021dropoutregularizerinteractioneffects}
that dropout acts as an order-specific regulariser, decaying the effective
learning rate of higher-order interactions and preventing the model from
fully exploiting the interaction structure present in the data.

Figure~\ref{fig:architecture_x_regularization} confirms that this pattern
holds across both the small and big architectures. The small model
consistently outperforms the big model under base and weight decay
conditions, while the two architectures converge under dropout, suggesting
that the capacity advantage of the big model is negated when dropout
prevents effective interaction learning. This architecture--regularisation
interaction is discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:arch_comparison}.

\subsection{Effect of Noise on Recovery}

Output noise reduces interaction recovery monotonically across all noise
levels, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:auprc_by_noise}. The degradation is
gradual at low noise levels --- pooled AUPRC drops by only 12.8\% and
13.2\% at $\eta = 0.1$ and $\eta = 0.2$ respectively --- but accelerates
at higher levels, reaching a \statNoiseDrop\ reduction at $\eta = 1$
relative to the noise-free condition (\statNoiseOneMean\ vs
\statNoiseZeroMean). The step between $\eta = 0.2$ and $\eta = 0.5$
($-$13.2\% to $-$24.7\%) is notably larger than the step between $\eta =
0$ and $\eta = 0.2$, suggesting a threshold effect where moderate label
noise is tolerated but high noise substantially impairs the model's ability to
encode interaction structure in its weights.

Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_by_noise} suggests that output noise primarily affects the
plateau height of the recovery trajectory rather than its shape. All noise
levels follow the same rapid early rise in the first 10--20 epochs, after
which trajectories diverge to different stable plateaus. This pattern is
consistent with the view that the interaction recovery process operates on its
own timescale in early training, and that output noise acts mainly as a ceiling
constraint on the final recovery level rather than fundamentally disrupting the
early recovery dynamics. This is
consistent with \cite{arpit2017closerlookmemorizationdeep}, who show that
noise fitting occurs as a later phase in training after real patterns have
already been learned.

Figure~\ref{fig:noise_effect_by_model_size} shows an asymmetry between
the two architectures that becomes more pronounced at high noise. At
$\eta = 0$, the big model marginally outperforms the small model (0.579
vs 0.549). However, at $\eta = 1$ this advantage reverses: the small
model achieves 0.395 against the big model's 0.325. This is consistent
with the larger model being more susceptible to label noise, potentially
because its greater capacity allows it to fit noisy targets more readily,
leaving less representational structure available for genuine interaction
recovery.

\subsection{Joint Effect of Noise and Regularization}

Figure~\ref{fig:summary_heatmap} presents the joint effect of noise level and
regularisation condition on mean AUPRC, averaged across all functions and both
architectures. The heatmap reveals that the two factors interact in a broadly
additive fashion: each independently degrades recovery, and the worst cell
combines both in their most damaging configurations.

The highest mean AUPRC is achieved by the base condition at zero noise (0.59),
and the lowest by dropout plus weight decay at noise~$= 1$ (0.36). Noise exerts
a stronger downward pressure than regularisation: moving from $\eta = 0$ to
$\eta = 1$ reduces mean AUPRC by approximately 0.20--0.27 points depending on
the regularisation condition, whereas moving from base to the worst
regularisation condition at a fixed noise level costs at most 0.25 points.

One result that deviates from the additive pattern is the weight decay column.
Unlike dropout, weight decay maintains relatively high recovery even at high
noise, reaching 0.47 at $\eta = 0.5$ and 0.32 at $\eta = 1$, both competitive
with or better than the base condition under the same noise levels. This
suggests that $L_2$ penalisation interacts differently with label noise than
dropout does, possibly because weight decay constrains weight magnitude without
disrupting the relative ordering of interaction path strengths that NID reads.

emerge clearly from the data.

First, the base condition reaches its peak latest at low noise ($40.0 \pm 17.4$

at $\eta = 0$). This ordering is consistent with the trajectory shapes in

gradually throughout training, whereas heavy regularisation forces an early
plateau. Dropout alone shows an intermediate pattern, peaking around epoch 16
at $\eta = 0$ and stabilising near epochs 34--42 for moderate noise levels.

condition. For the base and weight decay conditions, peak timing is relatively
stable across noise levels from $\eta = 0.1$ to $\eta = 0.5$, clustering in

$\eta = 0$ (16.1), suggesting that in the presence of heavy noise, dropout's
suppression of higher-order learning is partially offset by a slower,

\subsection{Optimizer Effect}

Adam consistently outperforms SGD on interaction recovery across all conditions.
Table~\ref{tab:auprc_by_optimizer} shows a mean AUPRC of \statAdamMean\ for Adam
versus \statSGDMean\ for SGD, a gap of 0.084 points. Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_by_optimizer}
reveals that this difference is not primarily a final-performance effect but a
trajectory effect: Adam reaches a high plateau within the first five epochs and
maintains it stably throughout training, while SGD rises more slowly and does not
converge to a comparable level even by epoch 100. The shaded bands show that both
optimisers exhibit similar cross-function variance, so the gap is not driven by
SGD failing on a small number of outlier functions.

The slower convergence of SGD is consistent with its well-known sensitivity to
learning rate scheduling. With a fixed learning rate of 0.01 across all 100
epochs, SGD does not benefit from Adam's adaptive per-parameter step sizes,
which appear to be particularly useful for the sparse gradient structure
produced by $L_1$ regularisation on the interaction weights. The practical
implication is that, for interaction detection on these benchmark functions at
the training budget used here, optimizer choice is a meaningful source of
performance variation comparable in magnitude to the effect of moderate noise.

$f_3$, $f_5$, $f_7$, $f_8$, $f_9$, $f_{10}$) while the big model leads on three
($f_2$, $f_4$, $f_6$). The margins vary considerably: for $f_5$ and $f_8$ the
small model outperforms the big model by 0.133 and 0.141 points respectively,
while for $f_2$, $f_4$, and $f_6$ the big model's advantages are modest (0.021,
0.030, and 0.039).

The consistent advantage of the small model on the higher-performing functions
($f_5$, $f_8$, $f_{10}$) is noteworthy. These are also the functions with the
largest standard deviations in Table~\ref{tab:auprc_by_function}, indicating that
they are the most sensitive to experimental conditions. The pattern is consistent
with the noise analysis in Section~\ref{sec:results}: greater model capacity
appears to increase susceptibility to label noise and regularisation, preventing
the big model from reliably reaching the high AUPRC values that the small model
achieves under favourable conditions. On the lower-performing functions ($f_1$,
$f_2$, $f_3$, $f_4$), differences between architectures are small in absolute
terms, suggesting that on structurally difficult functions, capacity alone is not
the limiting factor.

Taken together, the architecture comparison does not support a straightforward
recommendation in favour of deeper networks for interaction detection. The big
architecture offers no systematic advantage and incurs meaningful costs on
functions where interaction recovery would otherwise be high.

\subsection{AUPRC and Validation Loss}

Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_vs_val_loss} plots mean AUPRC against mean validation loss
at the best epoch for each function--architecture combination. The Spearman
correlation is \(\rho = \statSpearmanRho\), \(\statSpearmanP\), computed on all
individual convergent runs, with \(n = \statRetainedRuns\). The negative sign confirms
that lower validation loss is associated with better interaction recovery, but the
magnitude is modest: validation loss explains only a small fraction of the
variance in AUPRC.

The scatter makes this clear. Several function--architecture combinations achieve
high AUPRC (above 0.65) at low validation loss, while others with similarly low
validation loss remain near 0.25--0.40. In the mid-range of validation losses
($10^{-1}$ to $10^{0}$), the spread in AUPRC is almost as large as the full
observable range. This suggests that a model can converge to a low training
objective while encoding very different amounts of interaction structure in its
weights, depending on the function, the regularisation applied, and the
architecture.

The practical implication for interaction detection is that validation loss is a
weak proxy for interaction recovery quality and cannot be used reliably as a
selection criterion for the best checkpoint or the best experimental condition.
Evaluation against the ground truth, as done throughout this study, is necessary
to distinguish conditions that genuinely improve interaction recovery from those
that merely reduce the training objective.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This study set out to examine how regularisation techniques and output noise
jointly affect the trajectory and stability of interaction recovery in MLPs,
evaluated against known ground truth interactions. The results permit several
substantive conclusions, while also revealing a number of limitations that
constrain the scope of the findings.

\subsection*{Regularisation does not uniformly improve interaction recovery}

The most consistent finding across all conditions is that regularisation
does not improve interaction recovery relative to an unregularised baseline.
Dropout reduces mean AUPRC by \statDropoutDrop\ relative to the base
condition, and the combined dropout and weight decay condition produces a
nearly identical reduction of \statDropoutWDDrop. This is consistent with
\cite{lengerich2021dropoutregularizerinteractioneffects}, who show that
dropout acts as an order-specific regulariser that decays the effective
learning rate of higher-order interactions. In the context of interaction
detection, this means that the very mechanism intended to prevent
overfitting actively suppresses the weight structure that NID relies on to
identify interactions.

Weight decay presents a different picture. Its reduction relative to base
is modest ($-$7.1\%), and the heatmap in
Figure~\ref{fig:summary_heatmap} shows that weight decay maintains
competitive recovery even at high noise levels, outperforming base at
$\eta = 1$ in some conditions. This suggests that $L_2$ penalization
constrains weight magnitude without dismantling the relative strength
differences between interacting and non-interacting paths, which are what
NID ultimately reads.

The near-equivalence of dropout alone and dropout combined with weight
decay --- mean AUPRC of \statDropoutMeanAUPRC\ and \statDropoutWDMeanAUPRC\
respectively --- further supports the conclusion that dropout is the
dominant factor. Once dropout is applied, adding weight decay provides no
additional recovery, and the two conditions produce statistically
indistinguishable results across all noise levels.

\subsection*{Output noise lowers the recovery ceiling without changing early dynamics}

Output noise degrades interaction recovery monotonically, but the
trajectories in Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_by_noise} suggest that this
degradation is better characterized as a ceiling effect than as a
disruption of the recovery process itself. All noise levels share the same
rapid early rise in the first 10--20 epochs; the trajectories then diverge
to different plateaus that they maintain stably for the remainder of
training. This pattern is consistent with
\cite{arpit2017closerlookmemorizationdeep}, who show that models exposed
to noisy labels tend to learn real patterns first and fit noise as a later
phase. If the same ordering holds here, interaction structure is encoded in
the weights during early training before noise fitting begins to compete
for representational capacity.

The threshold-like degradation between $\eta = 0.2$ and $\eta = 0.5$
($-$13.2\% to $-$24.7\%) is noteworthy and may reflect a regime change
where noise variance begins to dominate the gradient signal. Below this
threshold, the model appears to tolerate label noise while still recovering
meaningful interaction structure. Above it, the signal-to-noise ratio
deteriorates to the point where interaction recovery is substantially
impaired. This interpretation is consistent across both architectures,
though the larger model crosses this threshold at lower noise levels, as
discussed below.

\subsection*{Validation loss is a weak proxy for interaction recovery}

One of the more practically relevant findings of this study is that
validation loss is a poor predictor of interaction recovery quality.
The Spearman correlation between the two quantities, computed across all
\statRetainedRuns\ convergent runs, is $\rho = \statSpearmanRho$
(\statSpearmanP). While the negative sign confirms that lower validation
loss is generally associated with better AUPRC, the magnitude is weak:
the scatter plot in Figure~\ref{fig:auprc_vs_val_loss} shows that models
with similar validation losses achieve AUPRC values spanning almost the
full observable range.

This finding has a direct implication for practitioners using NID or
similar weight-based detection methods. A model selected on the basis of
validation loss will not reliably be the model with the best interaction
recovery. The two objectives are only loosely coupled, and conditions that
reduce validation loss --- such as strong dropout regularisation --- can
simultaneously suppress the weight structure that makes interaction
detection possible. Evaluation against ground truth, where available, is
therefore a necessary complement to standard loss-based model selection.

\subsection*{Smaller models recover interactions more reliably under adverse conditions}

The architecture comparison reveals no systematic advantage for the deeper,
wider big model. The small model achieves higher mean AUPRC on seven of
ten benchmark functions, and the largest margins are precisely on the
functions where recovery is otherwise high ($f_5$, $f_8$, $f_{10}$). This
is consistent with the noise analysis: the big model's greater capacity
appears to increase its susceptibility to label noise and regularisation,
preventing it from reliably reaching the recovery levels that the small
model attains under favorable conditions. On structurally difficult
functions where mean AUPRC is low for both architectures, the difference
between them is small, suggesting that neither capacity nor simplicity is
the primary limiting factor in those cases.

This finding runs counter to the intuition that more expressive models
should recover more complex interaction structures more faithfully. It
instead suggests that, in the regime studied here, the interaction
detection signal is sufficiently weak that additional capacity provides
more opportunity to fit spurious patterns than to recover genuine ones.

\subsection*{Limitations}

Several limitations qualify the conclusions above. First, all trajectory
analyses are based on single-seed runs. The observed variability across
functions therefore conflates genuine structural differences between
functions with run-to-run randomness. Replicated experiments with multiple
seeds would be required to separate these sources of variance and support
stronger claims about stability.

Second, the study uses synthetic benchmark functions exclusively. These
functions were designed to test interaction detection under controlled
conditions, but they may not represent the difficulty or structure of
real-world datasets. Results should not be assumed to generalise directly
to applied settings.

Third, NID is treated as the fixed interaction detection instrument
throughout. The sensitivity of the findings to this choice is not assessed.
Alternative detection methods, such as those based on Shapley interaction
indices or neural additive models, may respond differently to the same
regularisation and noise conditions.

Fourth, the convergence filter described in Section~\ref{sec:trajectory_eval}
excludes runs where validation loss did not decrease by at least 3\%
across training. While this threshold is conservative, it may introduce
a selection bias toward conditions under which training is more stable,
potentially understating the failure rates of heavily regularised or
high-noise configurations in a true deployment setting.

Fifth, the evaluation criterion treats superset interactions as false
positives. While this is the strictest possible interpretation of exact
interaction recovery, it penalises cases where NID recovers the correct
feature set embedded within a larger proposed interaction, and may
underestimate partial recovery in higher-order interaction functions.

Finally, the noise manipulation in this study adds Gaussian noise to the
output variable $y$, not to the input features $x$. This is label or
output noise, and its relationship to regularisation is not the same as
for input noise. In particular, the formal equivalence between input
Gaussian noise and $L_2$ regularisation established by
\cite{Goodfellow-et-al-2016} does not apply here. The effects of output
noise on interaction recovery trajectories documented in this study should
therefore be understood as distinct from the dynamics that would arise
under input perturbation.

Several directions for future research follow directly from these limitations.
Replicating the experiment with multiple random seeds would allow genuine
function-level stability to be separated from run-to-run randomness, and
would permit statistical significance testing across conditions. Extending
the evaluation to real-world datasets with partially known interaction
structure --- for example, gene expression data where certain interaction
pairs have been validated experimentally --- would test whether the findings
transfer beyond synthetic benchmarks. Comparing NID against alternative
detection instruments, such as methods based on Shapley interaction indices
or contextual decomposition, under the same noise and regularisation
conditions would clarify whether the observed sensitivity is specific to
weight-based detection or reflects a more fundamental limitation of
interaction recovery under label noise. Finally, exploring input noise as
a distinct experimental factor, by perturbing features rather than labels,
would allow a direct empirical comparison between the two noise regimes
rather than relying on the theoretical equivalence that motivates combining
them.

\section{Conclusion}

This study examined how regularisation techniques and output noise jointly
shape the trajectory and stability of interaction recovery in multilayer
perceptrons, evaluated against known ground truth interactions using the NID
framework across ten synthetic benchmark functions.

The central finding is that regularisation does not uniformly support
interaction recovery. Dropout reduces mean AUPRC by \statDropoutDrop\ relative
to an unregularised baseline --- a reduction comparable in magnitude to moving
from zero to high output noise --- while weight decay is substantially more
benign at $-$7.1\%. The near-identical performance of dropout alone and dropout
combined with weight decay (\statDropoutMeanAUPRC\ vs \statDropoutWDMeanAUPRC)
confirms that dropout is the dominant suppressive factor, and that adding weight
decay once dropout is already applied yields no recovery benefit. These results
suggest that standard regularisation practice transfers poorly to the interaction
detection setting: the mechanisms that prevent overfitting in prediction tasks
can simultaneously prevent the network from encoding the weight structure that
NID relies on.

Output noise lowers the attainable recovery plateau without fundamentally
altering the early trajectory shape, consistent with the view that interaction
structure is encoded during the first 10--20 epochs of training before noise
fitting begins to compete for representational capacity. The degradation
accelerates markedly between $\eta = 0.2$ and $\eta = 0.5$, pointing to a
threshold beyond which the gradient signal is dominated by label noise. The
larger architecture is more susceptible to this effect, yielding lower recovery
than the small model at $\eta = 1$ despite marginal advantages at $\eta = 0$.

Perhaps the most practically consequential finding is that validation loss is a
weak proxy for interaction recovery quality (\(\rho = \statSpearmanRho\),
\(\statSpearmanP\)). Models with similar validation losses achieve AUPRC values
spanning almost the full observable range, implying that loss-based checkpoint
selection or condition ranking does not reliably identify the settings that best
support interaction detection. This decoupling between predictive and
interpretability objectives has direct implications for applied settings where
both goals are pursued simultaneously.

Taken together, these findings suggest that practitioners seeking to use
weight-based interaction detection should exercise caution when applying
standard regularisation schemes, prefer smaller over larger architectures when
interpretability is the primary goal, and evaluate interaction recovery directly
against available ground truth rather than relying on validation loss as a
surrogate. Future work replicated across multiple seeds, extended to real-world
datasets, and comparing alternative detection instruments would strengthen
these conclusions and clarify their scope.
